You have more rights than you use.

No one ever showed you how to use health care. You hear that the emergency room is the wrong place to go, but not what the right place is. A doctor may ask what you’d like to do. That doctor trained for a dozen years before being allowed to ask, and you get a few moments to answer.

Part of health care is the people who look after you, and they mostly want what you want. The other part is an industry, and it spends a lot of money on people you’ll never meet: a reviewer who rules on whether your care was necessary, a coder who settles what kind of visit you had, a collector who picks how many more times to ask. That side employs a revenue maximizer. Who’s your cost minimizer? The book shows you how to handle the people making those decisions, and how to get the most from the people treating you while doing right by them.

The book gathers them, explains the signs that something’s off, and supplies what to say next, whether you’re talking to a doctor, a billing office, an insurer or a pharmacist. Usually nothing is off, and it tells you that as well.

Eight things from the book

Here are eight findings, shortened, each with a sentence you can use. The sources, the exceptions, and the next move if the first sentence fails are in the book.

  1. Don’t pay the first bill.

    Hospital bills start out as totals. Before you pay, get the line-by-line version and wait for your insurer’s explanation of benefits. In a national survey, about three in four people who called about a bill they believed was wrong got it corrected, and 86 percent of those who never called figured it wouldn’t help.

    Say this

    “I got the bill for [date]. Before any payment, I need to see the itemized statement with procedure codes, along with your financial assistance policy and application.”

    In the book: Chapter 3, The Bill After, and the Debt

  2. Nonprofit hospitals have to offer a discount.

    Every nonprofit hospital has a written policy for lowering bills for patients who can’t pay, and the application window runs 240 days from the first bill. Only about three in ten of the patients it’s meant for end up getting it, and half say nobody at the hospital mentioned it.

    In the book: Chapter 3, The Bill After, and the Debt

  3. A denial is a first offer.

    In 2024, Medicare Advantage plans denied about four million requests for care. Only about one in nine denials was appealed, and 80.7 percent of those appeals succeeded in full or in part. Your appeal starts by asking for the file behind the decision.

    Say this

    “Citing 29 CFR 2560.503-1, I’m asking for these free of charge: the whole claim file, the exact clinical criteria or guideline behind the decision, the clinical reasoning, and the name and specialty of each medical professional you consulted.”

    In the book: Chapter 28, No: Who Decides, and How to Appeal

  4. Your chart is yours, in thirty days.

    Federal law gives you the right to your entire record, electronically, within thirty days, for no more than the cost of copying. Test results now land in your portal before your doctor has seen them.

    Say this

    “Under the HIPAA right of access, please send me my complete designated record set electronically within thirty days.”

    In the book: Chapter 8, Your Records Are Yours

  5. Ask for risk in people, not percentages.

    Say a drug takes your risk from 3 in 100 down to 2 in 100. That’s a one-third cut, which sounds big. It also means a hundred people take the drug so that one benefits. Both statements are accurate, but only the second tells you what you’re choosing.

    Say this

    “Picture a hundred people like me. Without the treatment, how many have this happen? With it, how many? Over how many years? How many get the side effect?”

    In the book: Chapter 12, Risk in Plain Numbers

  6. Doctors’ estimates of time left run long.

    When doctors predicted how long hospice patients would live, 63 of every 100 predictions were too hopeful and 17 were too gloomy. Across all of them, the guess averaged about five times too long. Ask for a range instead of a single number.

    Say this

    “For someone in my situation, what’s the likeliest outcome, and what are the best and worst cases?”

    In the book: Chapter 13, How Long Have I Got?

  7. “Your insurance won’t pay if you leave” is a myth.

    At one teaching hospital, seven in ten residents believed insurers won’t pay when a patient leaves against medical advice, and most of those who’d told patients so said it to keep them there. Ten years of the hospital’s billing records turned up no payment refused for that reason.

    In the book: Chapter 20, Getting Out, and Getting Better at Home

  8. A salesperson is usually in the operating room.

    When a device is implanted, someone from the manufacturer is often present. Shoulder surgeons, asked to estimate, put it at about four cases in five. The rep’s pay depends on what gets used. No state requires your consent to the rep’s presence, but you can still refuse.

    Say this

    “Please chart that I want the device representative kept out of the sterile field.”

    In the book: Chapter 17, Before the Operation

Should you buy it?

Probably, if

  • You’ve paid a medical bill you didn’t read, or nodded at a doctor and never asked what they meant.
  • An insurer told you no and you accepted it.
  • You’re about to turn 65, expecting a baby, living with a diagnosis, or looking after someone who is.
  • You’re healthy, insured, paying for it, and getting little use from it.

Probably not, if

  • You want someone to tell you which treatment to choose. The book explains the evidence and doesn’t pick for you.
  • You want an argument for or against a health system. It deals with the system you’re stuck with today.
  • You want a villain. There isn’t one here.
